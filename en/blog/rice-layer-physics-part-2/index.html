Research · The rice layer, part 2 of 3

The physics of the rice layer

Part 1 showed that cooks disagree profoundly about how much rice belongs in the pan. In this part I look at what physics has to say. Step by step I build a mathematical model of the pan: a water balance for the cooking phase and a heat model for the socarrat. I run it on the computer and check that the calculation is correct. The result is a set of predictions that we will test with real paellas in part 3.

Back to the blog

Note: this is a physics lesson.

This part is a substantial exercise in physics, with formulas, derivatives and numerical mathematics. I explicitly go through the steps by which a numerical model is developed, from energy balance to computer code. If you are only interested in the outcome, read the box below and feel free to go straight on to part 3, where we test the model against measurements and draw a conclusion.

In short
  • As long as the stock is boiling, everything in the pan is stuck at about 100 °C. In the cooking phase the question is therefore not how hot it is, but where the water goes.
  • The stock should be gone when the grain is cooked. The more rice per square centimetre, the more water has to evaporate in the same time. At 0.25 g/cm² that takes about 1.8 kW of net heat; at 0.71 g/cm², about 5 kW. An ordinary burner manages that only about half the time.
  • A thick layer therefore often fails to dry in time: the rice overcooks and turns mushy. A very thin layer on a normal fire dries too early, before the grain is cooked.
  • Once the pan runs dry, things move fast. At full fire the base reaches 150 °C (socarrat) after about 16 seconds and 200 °C (burning) after about 30 seconds. That window depends on the fire, not on the thickness of the layer.
  • These are predictions from a model with assumptions and uncertain inputs. Whether they hold, we test in part 3.

In part 1 I expressed the amount of rice as a single number: the rice density ρ, the mass of dry rice per square centimetre of pan area. The recipes ranged from 0.10 to almost 0.50 g/cm², and the pan trade went as high as 0.71. In this part I always calculate with four reference values: 0.10 (very thin), 0.25 (the Valencian middle zone), 0.45 (thick) and 0.71 (El Cid's capa llena), always in a 50-centimetre pan with a 45-centimetre base.

The approach follows the steps you learn in a course on numerical methods: first understand the physical problem, then set up the equations, turn them into something a computer can solve, check that the computer does it correctly, and only then look at the results. The Octave code I used for all the calculations can be downloaded at the bottom.

1

Making the question precise

A model is always an answer to a question. If you do not pose the question sharply, you build a model that tries to describe everything and describes nothing well. My questions are:

  1. Cooking phase: for a given rice density ρ, can the stock be gone at the moment the grain is cooked, and with how much fire?
  2. Socarrat phase: once the pan runs dry, how much time is there between socarrat forming and burning, and does that depend on ρ?

What I do not model: taste, the chemistry of the Maillard reaction, the cooking of meat and vegetables, and the sofrito beforehand. I also leave out the other ingredients in the pan. The model is about rice and stock.

2

Physical analysis: what happens in the pan?

Before writing down a single formula, it pays to name the processes and estimate their size. Five are at work in a paella:

  • Boiling: the burner heats the stock, which boils and therefore mixes vigorously.
  • Water uptake: the grain absorbs water and swells; the starch gelatinises. With Valencian rice this takes about 17 minutes.
  • Evaporation: water leaves the pan as vapour.
  • Heat conduction: heat creeps up from the base through the rice layer.
  • Heat loss to the air at the top.

The key lies in two numbers. Warming a kilo of water by one degree takes 4.2 kJ. Evaporating a kilo of water at 100 °C takes 2257 kJ, more than five hundred times as much. As long as there is liquid water, the temperature therefore cannot rise above 100 °C: all extra heat goes into evaporation. The boiling point acts as a thermostat.

How fast heat is conducted through a layer depends on the thermal diffusivity a=k/(ρc), with k the thermal conductivity, ρ here the density of the material and c the specific heat. For cooked rice a≈0.14 mm2/s. The time heat needs to creep through a layer of thickness L is of the order of

τ=aL2​.

The layer of rice left when the stock is gone is about 9 mm thick at 0.25 g/cm². Then τ≈10 minutes. At 0.71 g/cm² the layer is about 26 mm and τ≈80 minutes. A layer twice as thick needs four times as long. Pure conduction is slow. During cooking that is no problem, because the boiling stock pumps the heat around. But as soon as the pan is dry, only conduction can move the heat.

This analysis yields two phases, each with its own physics (figure 1). In the cooking phase everything is at about 100 °C and it is a matter of water bookkeeping. In the socarrat phase the water at the base is gone and it is a matter of heat conduction with evaporation.

4

Phase 1: the water balance of the cooking phase

Because everything in the cooking phase is at about 100 °C, the model does not need a temperature distribution here. It only follows water, for the whole pan at once. There are two clocks.

Clock 1: the grain. The rice absorbs water, quickly at first and then more and more slowly. I describe this as a first-order process:

ma​(t)=ma,max​(1−e−t/τs​),

with ma​ the water absorbed and ma,max​=1.6 kg of water per kg of rice. The time constant τs​=7.4 minutes is chosen so that after 17 minutes the grain has 90 percent of its water; that is what I call cooked.

Clock 2: the stock. The free stock mℓ​ decreases through absorption and through evaporation. As long as the stock is boiling, all net heat P from the burner goes into evaporation:

dtdmℓ​​=−dtdma​​−hfg​P​,hfg​=2257 kJ/kg.

Each kilowatt of net heat thus evaporates about 27 grams per minute. At the start, the cold rice does cool the stock to about 90 °C; that heat has to be put back first. The model includes that too, through a simple energy balance.

The ideal fire is the power P∗ at which the stock is gone exactly when the grain is cooked. It follows directly from the balance:

P∗=tcook​hfg​(mb​−0.9ma,max​)+Qreboil​​,

with mb​ the stock at the start (3 kg per kg of rice), Qreboil​ the heat needed to bring it back to the boil after adding the rice and tcook​ the cooking time of the grain. All terms are proportional to the amount of rice. The power needed per square metre is therefore proportional to ρ: twice as much rice per cm² needs twice as much fire per cm².

Why does this depend on the rice density? Because the stock scales with the rice. Every recipe works with about 3 kg of stock per kg of rice. Of that, the grain absorbs about 1.4 kg; the rest, some 1.6 kg per kg of rice, has to evaporate. More rice per cm² means more water per cm² that has to go, while the burner delivers a fixed amount of heat per cm² of base. Roughly:

tdry​≈q′′(water per m2)⋅hfg​​∝q′′ρ​.

The other clock, the cooking time of the grain, does not depend on the density: each grain sits in stock at 100 °C and is cooked after about 17 minutes, whether there is a lot of rice in the pan or a little. One clock shifts with the thickness, the other does not. That is why each thickness has only one matching fire.

Figure 2 shows the two clocks at a single fire setting. Figure 3 turns the question round: how much fire is needed for each density? Because the inputs are uncertain, I repeated the calculation 2000 times, each time with different values within their range: the amount of stock (2.5 to 3.5 kg per kg of rice), the water uptake (1.3 to 1.9 kg per kg), the cooking time (15 to 18 minutes), the pan diameter and the burner efficiency. Such a Monte Carlo analysis shows how the uncertainty in the inputs carries through into the outcome.

According to the manufacturer, a 40 cm burner delivers 11 to 17 kW of gas. How much of that actually reaches the pan is unknown; I use 20 to 50 percent. For 0.10 and 0.25 g/cm² that is always ample; you cook at half power or less. At 0.45 g/cm² it works in 86 percent of the draws, at 0.71 g/cm² in only 44 percent. A full layer therefore often needs more fire than the burner can deliver. The rice is then cooked while there is still stock in the pan, and it carries on cooking until it is mushy. That is exactly the Valencian tradition's complaint about thick paellas, now with a physical explanation.

This phase also explains an observation by the rice master José Luis Berlanga from part 1: in a larger pan the layer is thinner, the liquid evaporates faster and you need less stock. In the model it is the same proportionality with ρ.

Finally, the water balance gives the thickness of the layer at the moment the pan runs dry. Raw rice lies about 4 mm deep on the base at 0.25 g/cm²; swelling takes it to about 9 mm. At 0.71 g/cm² it goes from 11 to 26 mm. That layer is the starting point of the second phase. The heat model does not need to know that the layer grows during cooking: as long as everything is at 100 °C, the thickness makes no difference to the temperature.

5

Phase 2: what happens when the pan runs dry

At the end of the cooking phase the free stock is gone. The rice now lies as a layer of about 9 mm (at 0.25 g/cm²) on the base, saturated with water and at 100 °C throughout. The burner keeps burning. What happens in the layer now?

At the bottom, against the hot steel, the water evaporates from the grains. As long as there is water there, it stays at 100 °C. Only when the bottom layer is dry can the temperature rise and the socarrat form. If the rise goes too far, the rice burns. Above it there is still wet rice that stays at 100 °C.

Here we do need to know how the temperature is distributed over the height, and how it changes over time. For that we set up a model in the following steps: an energy balance with evaporation, boundary conditions, a grid, a time integration, and a check that the computer does it correctly.

6

The heat equation, with evaporation

Take a thin horizontal slice of rice between heights y and y+Δy, with area A (figure 4). For that slice an energy balance holds: the increase in energy equals what comes in through conduction, minus what goes out, plus what is added or removed inside:

[E(t+Δt)−E(t)]AΔy=[q(y)−q(y+Δy)]AΔt+sAΔyΔt.

Here E is the energy per cubic metre, q the heat flux in W/m² and s a source or sink in W/m³. Divide by AΔyΔt and let Δy and Δt go to zero:

∂t∂E​=−∂y∂q​+s.

Fourier's law. Heat flows from hot to cold, in proportion to the temperature gradient: q=−k∂T/∂y, with k the thermal conductivity.

Evaporation. The slice contains solids (starch, protein) and water. With sv​ kilograms of solids and w kilograms of water per cubic metre, the heat capacity is

C(w)=sv​csolid​+wcw​.

As long as the slice is below 100 °C, or dry, all heat goes into warming: ∂E/∂t=C∂T/∂t. At 100 °C with water inside, the heat goes into evaporation: the temperature stands still and the water decreases by

∂t∂w​=−hfg​1​(∂y∂​(k∂y∂T​)),hfg​=2257 kJ/kg.

Together this is the heat equation with a phase change. The standard way to solve it is called the enthalpy method: you keep track of the energy in each piece of layer and derive from it whether it warms up or evaporates water. The thermal conductivity depends on the water too: from 0.55 W/(m K) for wet rice to about 0.20 for dry.

7

Initial and boundary conditions

A differential equation on its own does not determine the solution. You also have to say how the state starts and what happens at the edges.

  • Initial condition. At the moment the pan runs dry, the layer is at 100 °C throughout and full of water. The thickness L and the water content come from phase 1.
  • Bottom: the burner. The burner supplies a fixed heat flux q′′ into the base. This is a Neumann boundary condition: −k∂T/∂y(0,t)=q′′. At full fire q′′≈28.6 kW/m² (about 4.5 kW net over the 45 cm base).
  • Top: the air. The layer loses heat to the air, more as it gets warmer. This is a Robin boundary condition: −k∂T/∂y(L,t)=h(T(L,t)−T∞​), with h≈10 W/(m²K) and T∞​=20 °C.

A third kind, the Dirichlet boundary condition, fixes the temperature itself. We will use it in part 3, when we feed the measured base temperature into the model.

8

Discretisation: the layer in cells

A computer cannot calculate with a continuous function T(y,t). We therefore divide the layer into N thin cells of height Δy (figure 5). For each cell we keep one temperature Tj​ and one water content wj​. Between two neighbouring cells, heat flows according to Fourier's law, with the temperature difference divided by the distance:

Fj+21​​=−kj+21​​ΔyTj+1​−Tj​​.

For k at the boundary between two cells I use the harmonic mean, so that the heat flux is right when a wet and a dry cell sit side by side. The energy balance per cell then becomes

ΔydtdEj​​=Fj−21​​−Fj+21​​.

For the interior cells this is exactly the familiar central difference for the second derivative, (Tj+1​−2Tj​+Tj−1​)/Δy2. The boundary conditions enter at the outer boundaries: at the bottom the flux is F21​​=q′′ from the burner, at the top the loss to the air. The partial differential equation has thus become a system of N ordinary differential equations, one per cell. This is called the method of lines. I use cells of an eighth of a millimetre; a 9 mm layer then has 73 of them.

9

Integration in time

Now the system has to be stepped forward in time, in steps Δt. I use the simplest scheme, explicit Euler: the new state follows directly from the old one,

Ejn+1​=Ejn​+ΔyΔt​(Fj−21​n​−Fj+21​n​).

After each step, every cell is checked to see whether its energy exceeds that of 100 °C while there is still water. If so, the excess goes into evaporation:

Δwj​=hfg​C(wj​)(Tj∗​−100)​,Tjn+1​=100 ∘C,

where Tj∗​ is the temperature the cell would reach without evaporation. Once the water is gone, the temperature simply keeps rising. That step is easy in an explicit scheme, which is why I chose one. Implicit schemes such as implicit Euler and the trapezoidal rule exist too, but become more complicated with a phase change.

Explicit Euler has one pitfall: it is only stable if the time step is small enough,

Δy2aΔt​≤21​,a=Ck​.

Choose a larger time step and small rounding errors grow with every step until the calculation blows up. Figure 6 shows this: just below the limit all is well, just above it the temperature swings between −150 and +240 °C within 35 steps. I therefore calculate with aΔt/Δy2=0.4, using the largest a found in the layer (that of dry rice). With cells of an eighth of a millimetre the time step is then about 0.014 seconds.

10

Verification: is the computer calculating correctly?

A program that produces numbers is not yet a program that produces the right numbers. Verification asks whether the computer solves the equations correctly. That is only possible if you already know the right answer somewhere. I do three checks.

A case with an exact answer. Switch off evaporation in the code and pure heat conduction remains. For a layer that receives a constant heat flux at the bottom, an exact formula then exists, as long as the heat has not yet reached the top (Carslaw & Jaeger):

T(y,t)=T0​+k2q′′​[πat​​e−y2/4at−2y​erfc(2at​y​)].

Figure 7 puts the calculation next to this formula: the lines coincide, both over time and over the height.

Grid convergence. The finer the cells, the smaller the error should become, and as theory predicts: with central differences the error becomes four times smaller when you halve Δy. This is called second order. Figure 8 shows exactly that: the largest error in the base temperature falls from 5.1 °C with half-millimetre cells to 0.09 °C with 1/16 mm cells. With evaporation switched on there is no exact formula, but the outcome converges there too: the time to 200 °C becomes 39, 32, 32 and 30 seconds as the cells get finer.

Energy balance. With evaporation on, the heat from the burner must reappear exactly as warming, evaporated water and loss to the air. After 60 seconds that holds to within 0.04 percent.

The conclusion: the program solves the equations correctly. Whether they are the right equations is another question. That is called validation, and it can only be answered with measurements. We do that in part 3.

11

Results: the socarrat

With the program checked, I can calculate the pan: a 9 mm layer (0.25 g/cm²) that has just run dry, with the burner at full fire.

Figure 9 shows the temperature against the base. With evaporation it takes 16 seconds to reach 150 °C and 30 seconds to reach 200 °C. In between there is a window of about 15 seconds in which the socarrat forms without the rice burning. That matches what Valencian cooks say: seconds separate a good socarrat from burnt rice.

For comparison, the same figure shows the calculation without evaporation: the base is then at 200 °C after just 14 seconds. The water in the grains roughly doubles the time to burning. Ignoring evaporation would give the wrong picture of the socarrat phase.

The steps in the curve are a known feature of the enthalpy method. The cells dry out one by one, and each time a cell is dry the rise briefly speeds up. The finer the cells, the smaller the steps.

Figure 10 looks inside the layer itself. A drying front creeps up from below. Below the front the rice is dry and the temperature rises steeply; above it the rice is wet and stays at 100 °C. After 30 seconds, when the base reaches 200 °C, only the bottom 0.7 millimetre is dry. The socarrat is therefore a very thin film, less than one grain thick, and the rest of the rice does not notice it.

Two outcomes of phase 2 strike me. First, the socarrat window hardly depends on the thickness of the layer: at all four densities it comes out at about 16 and 31 seconds. That makes sense, because only the bottom millimetre is involved. The thickness of the layer matters in phase 1, not in phase 2. Second, the window depends strongly on the fire (figure 11). At full fire you have about 15 seconds. At 11 kW/m², the cooking-phase fire at 0.25 g/cm², it takes 100 seconds for the socarrat to start, and you have almost two minutes of leeway. A high fire gives quick socarrat, but little margin.

12

What the model predicts

In summary, the model yields predictions that can be measured. That is exactly what part 3 is for:

  • Cooking phase. The net fire at which the stock is gone when the grain is cooked is proportional to the rice density: about 0.7 kW at 0.10 g/cm², 1.8 kW at 0.25, 3.3 kW at 0.45 and 5.2 kW at 0.71, for a 50 cm pan.
  • Too little fire with a lot of rice. At 1.8 kW net, the pan at 0.45 g/cm² only runs dry after about 28 minutes, and at 0.71 after about 43 minutes, long after the grain is cooked.
  • Too much fire with little rice. At 0.10 g/cm² and 1.8 kW the pan is dry after about 8 minutes, with the grain two-thirds cooked.
  • Socarrat. After running dry, at full fire the base reaches 150 °C after about 16 s and 200 °C after about 30 s, almost regardless of the layer thickness. On a lower fire the window becomes much wider.
  • Thickness of the socarrat. A fraction of a millimetre; the rest of the layer stays at 100 °C.

14

Limitations, and what part 3 has to measure

The model is only as good as its assumptions and its inputs. The main uncertainties, in order of influence:

  • Burner efficiency. How much of the gas actually reaches the pan? I used 20 to 50 percent. This decides whether a thick layer is feasible.
  • The water uptake of bomba. How much water does the grain absorb, and how fast?
  • The limits for socarrat and burning. 150 and 200 °C are guide values; the colour of the crust is easier to measure than to predict.
  • Even heating. A ring burner does not heat equally everywhere; then the pan does not run dry everywhere at once.
  • Other ingredients. Chicken, rabbit and beans take up space, water and heat. I left them out on purpose.

In part 3 we measure with scales, a row of thin thermocouples in the layer and real paellas with different amounts of rice. That is the validation. Where the model deviates, we adjust it and account for that here, in part 2.

Conclusion of part 2

Physics sharpens the controversy from part 1. In the cooking phase everything revolves around water. The stock has to be gone when the grain is cooked, and the fire needed for that grows in proportion to the amount of rice per square centimetre. Around 0.25 g/cm² a medium-high fire is enough. A full layer of 0.71 g/cm² often needs more than an ordinary burner can give, and the rice overcooks before the pan runs dry. A very thin layer, on the other hand, needs a low fire, or it dries out before the grain is cooked.

In the socarrat phase it is a matter of seconds, and those are set by the fire, not by the layer thickness. Whether this model describes reality well, we do not yet know. That is the question for part 3.
